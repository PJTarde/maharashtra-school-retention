UDISE+ · Maharashtra · 2021-22 to 2025-26

Where Maharashtra loses its students

Five years of school returns for every recognised school in the state — 1,08,139 schools, 2.16 crore children. Tracked grade by grade, almost none of them leave school before class 10. Then a fifth of them vanish at once, in every single year of the panel.

Source: UDISE+ Data Sharing Portal, state code 27 · up to 7 tables × 5 years · school identifiers are stable across the panel (99.3–99.7% carry-over year to year), so the same school is followed throughout

Finding 01 — the exit point

Ten years of near-perfect retention, then a cliff

80.3%of class 10 pupils in 2024-25 appear in class 11 the next year

Every other grade-to-grade step in Maharashtra holds between 99.6% and 102.6%. The step from class 10 into class 11 holds only 80.3% — a one-year loss of 3,49,816 children. It is not a one-year artefact: the cliff appears in all four transition pairs in the panel, at 76.1%, 80.4%, 79.6% and 80.3%. Over five years it has cost roughly 15 lakh school places.

Finding 02 — who leaves

At the cliff edge, boys leave more than girls — and the gap widens every year

The expected pattern in Indian school data is girls dropping out faster after elementary school. Maharashtra inverts it at the decisive moment: at the class 10 → 11 exit, girls are retained 4.5 percentage points more often than boys.

With five years on the panel this is no longer a single reading. The gap has grown in every consecutive pair — 1.7, then 4.0, then 4.2, now 4.5 points — because boys' retention has fallen while girls' has held. Whatever pulls pupils out of school after class 10 in Maharashtra is pulling increasingly hard on boys.

Finding 03 — the infrastructure gap

A rural government school is a different institution

Each school is scored on 11 basic facilities it either has or lacks — functional drinking water, functional toilets for each sex it teaches, electricity, library, playground, ramps, handwashing, a computing device, internet and medical check-ups. The score is the share of applicable items met, so a boys-only school is not marked down for having no girls' toilet.

Maharashtra's average rose from 82.8 to 92.2 over the five years — real, steady improvement. But the state average hides a gap of six and a half to seven and a half points between the 58,247 rural government schools and the private categories around them, and rural government schools teach 37 lakh children. The gap has narrowed since 2021-22, but it has not closed.

Finding 04 — geography

The two gaps do not line up

If poor facilities drove pupils out, the worst-equipped districts would lose the most children at class 11. They do not. Across the 35 districts where both can be measured, the correlation between infrastructure score and class 10 → 11 retention is r = 0.26 — weak, and in the counter-intuitive direction in the clearest cases: Jalgaon is among the best-equipped districts in the state and worst on retention (62.8%), while Gadchiroli ranks last on infrastructure yet retains above the state average.

So the cliff is not an infrastructure problem. Five years of spending moved the facility score up nine points and left it exactly where it was. The next section tests what does explain it.

Finding 05 — the mechanism

The cliff is a doorway problem: 55% of pupils have to change school to continue

There is a sharp test available. A pupil in a school that already runs class 11 can carry straight on. A pupil in a school that ends at class 10 must find a different institution — apply, travel, pay, and be admitted. The school records tell us which of the two every pupil is in.

55% of all class-10 pupils — 9.7 lakh children in 17,110 schools — sit in a school with no class 11 to go on to. Meanwhile the schools that do offer class 11 enrol 103.7% of the class 10 they themselves taught the year before, so they are net importers: pupils are moving towards them.

What this cannot show. A school identifier is not a pupil identifier, so a school's class-11 roll mixes its own continuing pupils with arrivals from terminal schools, and nothing in this data separates the two. That 103.7% is an intake ratio, not a continuation rate. The accounting identity alone bounds the two groups: between 64% and 100% of pupils who had to move found a class-11 place, and between 56% and 100% of those who could stay continued. It is consistent with the loss falling on either group. The one thing needing no assumption: 5,86,901 class-11 places sit in schools with no class 10 of their own, so those pupils certainly arrived from somewhere else. The block evidence below, which does not require tracking anyone, is what carries this finding.

What actually predicts whether a block keeps its pupils

Standardised regression coefficients across 407 blocks, so the bars are directly comparable. Longer bar = stronger effect. R² = 0.26.

Having nowhere to go is two and a half times the effect of infrastructure — and infrastructure carries the wrong sign, which is the Jalgaon paradox showing up as a coefficient.

One methodological point matters here. At district level this relationship nearly vanishes (r = −0.08), because pupils commute across blocks inside a district and the movement cancels out. At block level it is unmistakable. The district was simply the wrong unit — which is also why the correlation in the previous section was so weak.

Finding 06 — the early warning

Boys arrive at the cliff already behind

The age table — 7.8 million rows, unused until now — records every pupil's age alongside their grade. Nominal age for class N is N+5, so anyone older is repeating or started late. Over-age enrolment is the standard leading indicator of dropout, and it is rising: 5.4% of class-10 pupils in 2022-23, 7.8% now.

It is also strongly gendered, and the gender gap widens exactly where the retention gap widens. Boys are 2.2 points more likely than girls to be over-age in class 8; by class 12 the gap is 4.3 points. That is a plausible mechanism for Finding 02 — boys reach the class 11 doorway carrying more repetition, and repetition is what precedes leaving.

Across blocks, over-age share does predict lower continuation (β = −0.19) — about the same weight as infrastructure, well behind the doorway problem. One test failed and is worth stating. At individual school level over-age appeared to raise continuation, which is an artefact: schools that admit over-age pupils are receiving institutions, so their intake counts arrivals as well as their own pupils. The school-level measure cannot separate the two; only the block aggregate can.

Finding 07 — who falls off

Adivasi children lose the cliff by eighteen points

Splitting the class 10 → 11 step by social category gives the starkest number in this report. General-category pupils continue at 82.9%. Scheduled Tribe pupils continue at 64.9%. That is an 18-point gap, and it is not a fluke of one year — ST retention has been the lowest of the four groups in every transition pair in the panel.

It shows up as compositional bleed. ST children are 12.5% of class 1 and 8.2% of class 12, and more than half of that entire loss happens in the single step from class 10 to 11.

But the doorway does not explain it

The obvious hypothesis is that Finding 05 accounts for this — that ST pupils are stuck in schools that end at class 10. They are not. The share sitting in a terminal school is 55.6% for ST against 57.7% for General: essentially identical, and if anything slightly lower for ST. The doorway burden is spread evenly across every category.

Share of class-10 pupils in a school that ends at class 10

Near-identical across categories — so this is not the cause of the caste gap.

So there are two separate mechanisms. The doorway explains where the cliff is — which blocks lose pupils. Caste explains who goes over it. A useful clue for the second: ST class-10 pupils are 78% rural against 35% for General pupils, so even where a junior college exists somewhere in the block, it is a different journey.

The two compound where you would fear. In Jalgaon — already the worst district in the state at 62.8% — ST retention is 42.5%. In Dhule it is 46.3%. Nandurbar, where ST pupils are 72.7% of the class-10 cohort, retains 56.3%.

One last comparison puts the earlier gender finding in proportion. Girls out-retain boys inside every category — the gap is widest for SC pupils, at 6.8 points. But ST girls continue at 67.0%, still fourteen points below General boys at 81.2%. On this transition, caste outweighs sex by a wide margin.

Finding 08 — staffing

The teacher shortage is not where you would look for it

Across elementary schools the state's pupil-teacher ratio is comfortably inside the RTE norm of 30:1 — but the aggregate conceals two opposite failures. Urban private-aided schools run the heaviest classrooms in the state (35.9:1). Rural government schools run the lightest ratio (19.3:1) while containing 8,152 single-teacher schools — a school where one adult teaches every grade. That count has risen in every year of the panel, from 5,906 in 2021-22 to 9,269 statewide now, even as total teachers rose.

Finding 09 — new this year

The first school-safety return shows the sharpest divide in the data

2025-26 adds a table that has never been collected before: school safety. Fire extinguishers, disaster plans and teacher ID cards are close to universal. The measures that need money or trained staff are not.

CCTV splits the state almost perfectly along the line this report keeps finding. 92.6% of urban private unaided schools have cameras; 27.7% of rural government schools do — a 65-point gap, the widest of any indicator in five years of data. Only 25.4% of schools report self-defence training for girls, and only 43% have a teacher trained as a first-level counsellor.

Method

What the data does and does not support

Six tables per year (seven in 2025-26) — profile, facilities, teachers, safety and three enrolment files — joined on the school identifier into one panel of 5,42,682 school-year rows across three different UDISE+ release schemas, plus 7.8 million rows of age-by-grade enrolment. The age table reconciles exactly with the headcount table for every grade, which is the check that it is joined correctly. Regressions are ordinary least squares on 407 blocks with at least 200 class-10 pupils. Enrolment totals are built from the four social categories (General, SC, ST, OBC), which partition every pupil exactly once. The rebuilt totals reproduce Maharashtra's published UDISE+ figures (2.16 crore pupils, PTR 28.8 in 2025-26), which is the check that the joins are right.

SchemaThree release formats across five years

2021-22 misspells the key column as psuedocode, keys enrolment by a text label instead of numeric codes, stores one drinking-water flag where later years store six, and omits the teacher caste breakdown. 2022-23 to 2024-25 share one schema. 2025-26 changes again: facilities gain 12 columns, profile gains 11, and enrolment splits transgender pupils into their own *_t column — earlier years folded them into *_b, so they are folded back here to keep the series continuous (statewide that is 1–2 pupils).

ReclassificationManagement code 7 is not what it looks like

In 2025-26 management code 7 jumps from 79 schools to 3,402. Tracking those schools back one year shows 2,359 were Private Aided and 962 Private Unaided, so it is a private-sector residual category, not a government one. It is kept in its own group here; folding it into “Government” on the strength of a standard code list would have moved 3,300 private schools across this report's central comparison.

Comparability2021-22 totals, and two suspiciously perfect trends

Total enrolment falls from 2.26 crore to 2.12 crore between the first two years, pushing every 2021-22 transition down to roughly 94%. That is a reporting-basis change, not 14 lakh children leaving school in one year, so headline retention uses the latest pair only. Two later series need the same scepticism: the “not applicable” water code was retired in 2024-25, so functional drinking water reaching 100% is a coding change rather than an overnight fix; and schools recording zero teachers fall from 943 to 9, which is data validation, not hiring. Single-teacher schools — which rose over the same period — are the more trustworthy staffing measure.

TrackingSchools can be followed; pupils cannot

pseudocode is a school identifier, not a pupil one, and there is no pupil-level file. Every “retention” figure here is therefore a ratio of two headcounts a year apart, never a followed cohort. This matters most in Finding 05: a school's class-11 roll mixes its own continuing pupils with arrivals, so no school-level ratio can be read as a continuation rate. Only aggregate bounds and the block model survive that limitation, and only those are relied on.

CausationWhat the block model does and does not show

Block retention counts pupils who reach class 11 in that block, so a pupil who commutes to the next block registers as a local loss. The block model therefore establishes that local supply drives local continuation; it does not by itself prove that the 3.5 lakh statewide shortfall is dropout. The statewide figure is the one that nets out commuting, and it is the one quoted as the loss. Over-age enrolment for 2021-22 reads 26.4% against 5–8% in every later year, so that year is excluded from the age series along with everything else about it.

CohortsAggregate, never school-by-school

Maharashtra's schools mostly end at class 4 or 7, so pupils change school at those boundaries. Tracking a cohort within one school would count a normal transfer as a dropout. All retention is computed on state or district totals, where transfers net out.

DistrictsThree renames, one gap

Aurangabad became Chhatrapati Sambhajinagar, Osmanabad became Dharashiv, and Ahmadnagar became Ahilyanagar in 2025-26; all three are merged here, each confirmed by matching school counts. Mumbai (Suburban) reports no class 11–12 at all — its junior colleges sit under Mumbai II, which is why Mumbai II shows a retention above 100% and Mumbai (Suburban) shows none.

ExcludedColumns that could not be made comparable

furniture_availability is a count of furniture sets in 2021-22 (0–18,777) and a 1/2/3 code from 2022-23 onward, so no single test works across the panel. boundary_wall and building_status are type codes whose labels appear in no supplied schema PDF. All three are left out of the infrastructure index rather than guessed at.

LimitsWhat is not in this data

School identifiers are pseudonymised and there are no coordinates, so no school can be named or mapped below district level. There are no learning outcomes, no attendance and no finance beyond a grant receipt flag — so “retention” here means appearing in the next grade's count, not passing anything.
